## Lesson 1: The Consonants

Source page: 21

### Revision concepts

- Hebrew has twenty-three consonants and is read from right to left.
- Five letters have special word-final forms: ך, ם, ן, ף, ץ.
- The six בגדכפת letters may take דָּגֵשׁ קַל; in ב/בּ, כ/כּ, and פ/פּ the dot changes pronunciation.
- Several consonants share sounds; recognition must therefore depend on letter shape as well as pronunciation.
- א, ה, ח, and ע are gutturals and behave specially in later vocalization rules.

### Vocabulary

No separate vocabulary items are introduced in this lesson.

## Lesson 2: The Vowels

Source page: 26

### Revision concepts

- Vowel signs appear under, over, or after the consonant whose sound they follow.
- Recognize the principal values: patach/qamets = a; segol = e; tsere = ey; chireq = i; qamets-chatuf/cholem = o; qibbuts/shureq = u.
- קָמֶץ normally represents a, but in certain environments represents o as קָמֶץ חָטוּף.
- ה, ו, and י may participate in writing long vowels.
- Read a consonant-vowel unit as a single syllabic sequence rather than naming its signs separately.

### Vocabulary

No separate vocabulary items are introduced in this lesson.

## Lesson 3: שְׁוָא (Shava)

Source page: 29

### Revision concepts

- A vocal שְׁוָא is pronounced as a very brief vowel and is transliterated ĕ.
- שְׁוָא is vocal at the beginning of a word and when it is the second of two consecutive shevas.
- In other positions it is normally silent.
- Under gutturals, שְׁוָא is commonly replaced by a composite sheva: חֲטַף־פַּתַח, חֲטַף־סֶגוֹל, or חֲטַף־קָמֶץ.

## Lesson 4: דָּגֵשׁ (Dagesh)

Source page: 31

### Revision concepts

- דָּגֵשׁ קַל occurs only in בגדכפת letters, normally when no vowel or vocal sheva precedes.
- דָּגֵשׁ חָזָק marks a lengthened or doubled consonant.
- דָּגֵשׁ חָזָק may occur in any consonant except the gutturals and ר.
- In ב, כ, and פ, either kind of dagesh produces the stop pronunciation.

### Vocabulary

No separate vocabulary items are introduced in this lesson.

## Lesson 6: Copular Clauses

Source page: 34

### Revision concepts

- Hebrew often omits the copula; context supplies is, was, or will be.
- A form of הָיָה may serve as an overt verbal copula.
- A third-person subject pronoun may serve as a substitute copula.
- The pronominal copula agrees with the subject in gender and number.
- Distinguish a null copula from a missing translation: English usually requires an expressed form of “to be.”

## Lesson 7: Nouns: Singular

Source page: 36

### Revision concepts

- Most nouns are derived from a three-consonant שֹׁרֶשׁ and inflect for gender and number.
- Masculine singular nouns generally have no overt gender ending.
- Feminine singular nouns frequently end in ־ָה or ־ֶת/־ַת.
- Some feminine nouns, especially paired body parts and אֵם, have no overt feminine marker and must be memorized.
- Gender governs agreement with pronouns and copulas.

## Lesson 8: The Article הַ and the Interrogative הֲ

Source page: 38

### Revision concepts

- The article marks a noun as definite; Biblical Hebrew has no indefinite article.
- The normal article consists of הַ plus דָּגֵשׁ חָזָק in the noun’s first consonant.
- The interrogative הֲ attaches to the beginning of a clause and marks a yes-no question.
- Do not confuse article הַ, which modifies a noun, with interrogative הֲ, which marks the clause.

| בַּיִת (בָּתִּים) | bayit (battim) | house | noun M; irregular plural shown |
| מָה | ma | what? | interrogative |

## Lesson 9: לְ of Possession: Singular

Source page: 39

### Revision concepts

- Possession may be expressed by a copular clause built with לְ: literally “to X is Y,” idiomatically “X has Y.”
- לְ may take attached pronouns to express has/his/hers/yours/mine.
- When לְ, בְּ, or כְּ attaches to a definite noun, it replaces the article’s ה but retains the article’s vowel and dagesh.
- Parse the לְ phrase as possessor, not as the possessed item.

- The regular masculine plural ending is ־ִים and the regular feminine plural ending is ־וֹת.
- The dual ending ־ַיִם commonly marks natural pairs.
- Paired body parts are grammatically feminine.
- Frequent nouns may have irregular plurals whose vowels or stems differ from the singular; memorize the printed pair.
- A plural ending does not always reliably reveal lexical gender.

## Lesson 12: יֵשׁ and אֵין

Source page: 46

### Revision concepts

- יֵשׁ expresses positive existence: there is/are.
- אֵין expresses negative existence: there is/are not.
- Both may function as copular expressions.
- Translate idiomatically according to context rather than preserving Hebrew word order mechanically.

## Lesson 13: Conjunction וְ and Prepositions בְּ, כְּ, לְ, and מִן

Source page: 48

### Revision concepts

- וְ connects words and phrases and may also mark the beginning of a new clause.
- בְּ, כְּ, and לְ attach directly to the following word.
- מִן means from/out of and may also express comparison, “more than.”
- Before a normal consonant, attached מִן commonly assimilates its nun and places דָּגֵשׁ חָזָק in the following consonant.
- מִן may instead remain a separate word joined by מַקֵּף; it does not assimilate before a guttural.

- Questions asking for circumstances begin with an interrogative word such as who, what, why, how, when, or where.

## Lesson 15: Verbs: A Preview

Source page: 53

### Revision concepts

- A verb is parsed by root, בִּנְיָן, conjugation, person, gender, and number.
- The seven primary binyanim are Qal, Nifal, Piel, Pual, Hitpael, Hifil, and Hofal.
- A verb’s meaning cannot be predicted reliably from its root and binyan; each lexical verb must be learned.
- The Perfect gives a whole/perfective view and commonly, though not inherently, refers to past time.
- The Imperfect gives a partial/in-progress view and commonly, though not inherently, refers to nonpast time.
- Past Narrative, Participle, and Imperative/Jussive are introduced as secondary conjugations.

## Lesson 16: קַל Perfect Conjugation: Singular

Source page: 55

### Revision concepts

- The Perfect is perfective aspect: it portrays an action or event as a whole, although it can occur with past, present, or future reference.
- The 3ms Perfect has no person suffix and is the standard lexical citation form.
- Singular Qal Perfect endings are: 3ms zero, 3fs ־ָה, 2ms ־ְתָּ, 2fs ־ְתְּ, and 1cs ־ְתִּי.
- These person-gender-number suffixes apply throughout the binyanim, though stem vowels may change.
- הָיָה is frequent and irregular; learn its singular Perfect forms separately.

## Lesson 17: לְ of Possession: Plural

Source page: 58

### Revision concepts

- Plural pronominal suffixes attached to לְ express their/your/our possession.
- Distinguish masculine and feminine forms in both third and second persons.
- בְּ, כְּ, and מִן may also combine with pronominal suffixes; with suffixes, כְּ becomes כְּמוֹ־ and מִן becomes מִמֶּ־.
- A suffixed לְ phrase in a copular clause may express possession, while in a verbal clause it remains an ordinary prepositional phrase.

## Lesson 18: Word Order

Source page: 60

### Revision concepts

- A sentence has a subject and predicate; the predicate is usually verbal but may be nonverbal.
- An overt subject may be absent because Hebrew permits a null subject.
- Basic order is Subject-Verb, but Hebrew word order is flexible.
- Verb-Subject order commonly follows a fronted element; this inversion does not occur in participial or null-copula clauses.
- Relatives, interrogatives, negatives, and most grammatical words trigger Predicate-Subject order; וְ and עַתָּה generally do not.

### Vocabulary

| Hebrew | Transliteration | English gloss | Grammar / forms |
|---|---|---|---|
| אָכַל | ʾākhal | eat | Q |

## Lesson 20: סְמִיכוּת (Bound Nouns)

Source page: 64

### Revision concepts

- Hebrew expresses an 'of' relationship with a construct chain: נִסְמָךְ (bound/construct noun) followed by סוֹמֵךְ (host noun).
- The two nouns function as one compound and are frequently joined by maqqef.
- Use the appropriate construct form; especially recognize masculine plural/dual construct endings and feminine construct changes.
- The construct noun cannot take the definite article.
- The construct noun inherits definiteness from a definite host, such as a proper noun or an article-marked noun.

### Vocabulary

- A polysemous word has related senses; choose the suitable sense from context rather than importing every dictionary gloss.
- Valency patterns, especially the number and type of complements, help disambiguate senses.
- Lexical collocations further distinguish senses, such as reading a book versus shouting an exclamation.
- Compare near-synonyms by both their overlap and their restrictions; related Hebrew speech verbs do not all permit senses such as naming or reading.
